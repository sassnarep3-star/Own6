Bottom line

Do not serve either increase notice from the information currently available. The rent board’s published annual cap for increases effective March 1, 2026–February 28, 2027 is 1.6%, but eligibility, anniversary dates, prior increases/banked amounts, Housing Inventory/license status, lawful base rent, and the identity/continuity of tenants must be confirmed first. 1.6% is a legal ceiling for the annual allowance, not a recommendation or a finding that an increase is “reasonable.”

Executive summary

The supplied facts say both units are rent-controlled and in a building built in 1940. Treat these as owner-provided and not independently verified. A building’s age alone does not establish every legal fact or exemption; confirm coverage and unit records with the SF Rent Board. San Francisco’s local annual allowance is generally the appropriate starting point for these controlled tenancies. State law may also apply, but its cap and exemptions cannot be resolved without ownership, occupancy, unit, and exemption facts. There is no general federal annual rent cap identified in the official materials cited here; federal fair-housing protections still prohibit discrimination.

Prudent process / normal practice

  1. Obtain the Rent Board property/unit record, Housing Inventory compliance and current rent-increase license status; confirm coverage.
  2. Reconstruct the lawful base rent and full rent-change history separately for each tenancy, including prior annual increases, banked amounts, passthroughs and relevant service charges.
  3. Confirm which tenants hold the tenancy after April 1, 2026 and whether this was a roommate replacement. SF says rent cannot be increased just because a roommate is added/replaced; special treatment may apply if all original tenants have permanently left and only later subtenants remain.
  4. Use only an available, legally authorized increase; issue a written notice stating dollar amount, percentage and effective date. Generally at least 30 days; 90 days where the increase alone or combined with increases in the prior 12 months exceeds 10%; add five days for service by mail. Check service method and current forms with Rent Board.
  5. Apply the same documented, neutral policy to similarly situated tenancies. Different dates, lawful bases, histories, and eligibility can justify different outcomes; increasing both units merely to appear even-handed is not a legal safe harbor.

What is “reasonable”?

For these reported rent-controlled units, a conservative and ordinarily defensible annual increase is no more than the applicable SF annual allowance, only when due and all prerequisites are met. Larger increases may require a specific Rent Board-authorized basis/petition and evidence (for example, certain capital improvement or operating-cost petitions); market rents, inflation, taxes, repairs, or “keeping up with market” do not by themselves authorize a higher controlled rent. A small or zero increase can be reasonable. The rate for a later effective date may differ; re-check the official rate then.

Owner types & fairness

Large property operators commonly use centralized rent ledgers, scheduled annual review, template notices, and compliance controls; individual owners may manage these manually or through a property manager. These are general operational patterns, not verified claims about any named company or owner. Neither scale nor practice changes the legal cap. The safest approach for any owner is consistent written criteria, a unit-by-unit audit, documented calculations and neutral treatment. The fact pattern does not establish discrimination. Avoid decisions based on protected characteristics, retaliation, or a tenant’s exercise of legal rights; keep a contemporaneous business record.

Free assistance / independent review

Before serving a notice, contact the SF Rent Board for procedural guidance and ask a tenant-side attorney or qualified housing counselor to review the documents. The City’s tenant resources page lists counseling and legal-service options; eligibility and availability vary. No website can guarantee a tenant lawyer will approve an increase or predict litigation.
