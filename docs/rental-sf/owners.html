HOW OWNER TYPES SET RENTS

Big corporations vs individual owners — same cap, different machinery

What large real-estate corporations do

Large multifamily owners and managers have, since the 2010s, widely used algorithmic revenue-management software to set asking rents — most prominently RealPage. That practice drew a federal antitrust case:

EventWhat the official source saysSource
2024-08-23 — DOJ + 8 states sue RealPageDOJ alleged RealPage’s software used nonpublic, competitively sensitive data shared by landlords to train and run pricing recommendations; alleged Sherman Act §§ 1–2 violations; ~80% revenue-management software share; complaint sought to end the practice.S13
2025-11-24 — Proposed settlement filedIf approved: RealPage must stop using competitors’ nonpublic data at runtime; restrict training to data aged ≥12 months; no sub-state geographic model effects; remove features that limited price decreases/aligned pricing; court-appointed monitor; cooperation in suits against landlords.S14
  • The cap applies to corporations exactly as to anyone else. In this 1940 SF building, a Blackstone-scale owner could lawfully take only the 1.6% AGA (plus licensed banking) — no algorithm, model or “revenue optimization” can set a higher lawful figure.
  • Corporate increases in SF look like: automatic annual AGA applied portfolio-wide, software-driven initial rents on vacancy (legal), systematic passthrough petitions (capital improvements) where applicable, and growth concentrated on turnover, not sitting tenants (vacancy decontrol).
  • Corporate risk profile: antitrust exposure (RealPage), regulatory scrutiny of algorithmic pricing, class actions by tenants alleging coordinated inflation — none of which licenses a SF landlord to exceed the AGA.

What individual San Francisco owners do

  • Typical pattern (analysis, no official statistics exist): small owners commonly take the AGA each year (a tiny dollar amount — e.g., $50.40 on $3,150), or skip increases for years and later bank them (both scenarios are common in long-tenure SF stock).
  • Same obligations: Housing Inventory reporting (since 2023-03-01 for condos and <10-unit buildings), rent increase license, 30/90-day notice, anniversary mechanics, no rounding — identical for the owner of a duplex and a REIT [S06].
  • Practical differences, not legal ones: corporations automate notice calendaring, track AGAs centrally, file petitions at scale, and negotiate institutional financing; individual owners usually self-manage, often with an attorney or the Rent Board counselor for edge cases.

There is no official dataset comparing corporate vs individual increase behavior in SF; the qualitative pattern above is labeled analysis. See flag F-12.

What “normal business practice” looks like in SF

For a tenant-side reviewer, a normal practice is: (1) report into the Housing Inventory every March 1; (2) raise rent once per 12 months by at most the AGA (or a documented banked amount); (3) serve a correct 30/90-day written notice itemizing any banked portion; (4) keep proof of service and a clean rent ledger; (5) never single out a tenant, never retaliate, never exceed the cap without a petition. The uniform 1.6% course proposed on the Overview meets every element.
